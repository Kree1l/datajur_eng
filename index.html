At the Limits of Audience Engagement: When Does Interest in Netflix Series Begin to Decline

The Impact of Increasing Episode Runtime and Season Scale on Global Audience Engagement: A Case Study of Stranger Things and The Umbrella Academy (2016–2026)

Does the increasing runtime and expanding scale of Netflix series enhance audience engagement, or does it ultimately diminish viewers’ interest? Using Stranger Things and The Umbrella Academy as case studies, this research examines audience ratings, viewership data, and search activity between 2016 and 2026 to identify the factors that most effectively sustain audience engagement — whether extended viewing time, large-scale marketing campaigns, or the strength of the creators’ artistic vision.

Why Does It Matter

The aim of this study is to examine how changes in the production format of Netflix series — specifically episode length, total season runtime, the number of episodes, and the expansion of the directing team — influence audience engagement. Audience engagement is assessed through three key indicators: audience ratings, viewership, and online search activity.

The study is based on the hypothesis that increasing a season’s runtime and production scale does not necessarily lead to higher levels of audience engagement and may, under certain conditions, coincide with its decline.

Previous Research

The study An Experimental Examination of Binge Watching and Narrative Engagement (2019), examines the relationship between binge watching and audience engagement. Using an experimental design, the authors demonstrate that watching multiple episodes consecutively strengthens viewers’ emotional engagement, narrative transportation, and parasocial relationships with fictional characters. At the same time, the study suggests that these effects do not necessarily translate into sustained long-term engagement, highlighting the distinction between immediate emotional involvement and enduring audience interest.

A study published in Quantitative Marketing and Economics (2024) demonstrates that the relationship between watching intensity and audience engagement is non-linear. These findings reveal an inverse U-shaped pattern, with moderate levels of viewing intensity generating the highest levels of engagement, whereas excessive viewing may reduce audience interest and engagement.

While previous research has examined binge-watching and viewing intensity, less attention has been paid to whether changes in the scale and runtime of individual streaming series affect audience engagement across multiple seasons. This study addresses this question by combining episode-level data with a comparative analysis of two long-running Netflix series.

Research Methodology

This study employs a comprehensive analytical approach to examine the relationship between the production characteristics of television series and audience engagement. The analysis focuses on Stranger Things and The Umbrella Academy, selected as comparable case studies due to their shared genre, distribution platform, and multi-season narrative structure

Data were collected from multiple sources:

  • Google Trends data were used to assess changes in audience interest over time through online search activity;
  • User ratings and vote counts were collected from IMDb and comparable review platforms;
  • Content characteristics (episode runtime, number of episodes, season structure, composition of the directing team).

The collected data were cleaned and aggregated:

  • Average ratings were calculated for both individual episodes and entire seasons;
  • Audience engagement indicators (e.g., Google Trends indices) were normalized to ensure comparability across data sources;
  • Standardized metrics were constructed, including average episode runtime, average number of user votes, and other comparable measures.

The following analytical methods and tools were employed:

  • Descriptive statistics were used to identify overall trends in the data;
  • Pearson correlation analysis was conducted to examine the relationship between episode runtime and audience ratings;
  • Comparative case study analysis was used to identify both recurring and divergent patterns across the two series.

This framework was used to examine relationships between production scale and audience engagement while considering contextual factors such as marketing, final-season status, and broader media events.

Context

By the time their final seasons were released, both Stranger Things and The Umbrella Academy were established Netflix series, with both reaching the platform’s Top 10 during their first week of release.

Stranger things

During the first half of 2016, public interest in Stranger Things remained at its lowest recorded level, reaching just 1 point on the Google Trends index. This is unsurprising, given that the series premiered on July 15, 2016, and had originally been announced in April 2015 under its working title, Montauk, as reported by Variety.

Search interest reached its highest level in January 2026, nearly a decade after the series premiered. This indicates that Stranger Things was able to generate renewed audience attention across multiple release cycles rather than concentrating interest around its initial launch.

The mean search interest index is approximately 9.45, compared with a median of 5, indicating a positively skewed distribution. Audience attention was therefore concentrated around major release periods, with sharp peaks surrounding new seasons followed by longer periods of lower but persistent interest.

However, the peak observed in 2026 shouldn’t be interpreted as an absolute positive record driven solely by the series’ quality or high levels of fan appreciation, as such indicators are not measured within this dataset. It is important to consider that audience interest may also have been influenced by external factors, including the COVID-19 pandemic, which accelerated the growth of streaming consumption in 2020–2021, as well as broader social trends and media discussions surrounding cast members. For example, in August 2022, one month after the release of the fourth season, one of the main cast members of Stranger Things, Millie Bobby Brown, gave an interview with Allure in which she spoke about alleged harassment she had experienced from a former boyfriend. The interview received significant media attention, which may have contributed to increased public interest in the series during this period.

Episode Ratings: What Happened

The fifth season was widely perceived as the culmination of a decade-long narrative. Over the course of those ten years, Stranger Things evolved from a nostalgic science-fiction series into a global cultural phenomenon. Final episodes of long-running series often become media events in their own right: viewers who disengaged during earlier seasons may return, social media discussion intensifies, search activity increases, and media outlets publish analyses, recaps, and theories. As a result, audiences do more than simply watch an episode — they actively register their engagement through ratings, votes, and online discussion.

Following the release of the penultimate episode of the final season, rumors began circulating online about allegedly deleted scenes. In an attempt to draw the attention of the series’ directors and writers, fans launched a petition calling for the unreleased footage to be made public. The controversy was also accompanied by coordinated attempts to lower the episode’s rating through negative voting.

Positioned midway through the third season, the episode with the fewest user votes appeared at a point when the initial excitement had already subsided, while the narrative tension leading into the finale had yet to reach its peak.

The lowest rating was received by the previously discussed episode “Chapter Seven: The Bridge.” First, the low score may reflect unmet audience expectations: previous seasons had set a high benchmark, the series was approaching its conclusion, yet some viewers perceived the narrative as progressing too slowly. Second, both media outlets and fans criticized the episode for devoting excessive attention to characters who had played relatively minor roles elsewhere in the series, as well as for the performances of Millie Bobby Brown and Finn Wolfhard as Eleven and Mike. Third, a substantial portion of the episode is devoted to a scene in which Will Byers (Noah Schnapp) comes out as gay to his friends. The scene became a particularly contentious point in the episode’s reception. Some viewers accused Netflix of promoting a “gay agenda” to younger audiences and criticized the Duffer Brothers for, in their view, prioritizing politics over storytelling.

The highest rating was received by Season 4, Episode 7. For the first time in the series, the fourth season was not released in its entirety on a single day, but divided into two volumes. Episode 7 therefore served as the conclusion to the first volume, generating anticipation for the final two episodes. The episode also reveals the identity of the season’s main antagonist through a major plot twist, which may have further contributed to its strong audience reception.

An analysis of the series’ runtime shows that Season 1 had the shortest average episode runtime, at approximately 49 minutes. This is characteristic of many first seasons, when a series has yet to expand in scale and develop more complex narrative structures. At the same time, Season 1 received one of the highest average audience ratings, at 8.7, suggesting that relatively compact episodes were compatible with strong audience reception.

Episode runtimes increased across subsequent seasons, with the most substantial shift occurring in Season 4, when the average episode length reached approximately 84 minutes. Despite this considerable increase, Season 4 maintained a high average audience rating of 8.6. This suggests that longer runtimes don’t necessarily lead to lower audience engagement, provided the content continues to meet viewers’ expectations.

Season 5, however, had an average episode runtime of approximately 77 minutes and saw a substantial decline in its average rating to 7.9. This suggests that longer runtimes, when not supported by sufficiently substantial content and tightly structured storytelling, may make episodes feel unnecessarily drawn out. The decline may also reflect issues with pacing and narrative development, which could have contributed to audience disappointment, particularly given the heightened expectations surrounding the conclusion of the series.

The Pearson correlation coefficient between episode runtime and average rating is 0.097. It indicates an extremely weak positive correlation — effectively, little to no linear relationship between the two variables. An episode with a runtime of approximately 50 minutes has an expected average rating of around 8.3, while even at 120 minutes, the predicted rating remains within roughly the same range, at 8.3–8.4. On the rating scale used by streaming audiences, this difference is practically negligible.

What About the Directors

First, Andrew Stanton has the highest average rating, although he directed only two episodes. With such a small sample, however, the result cannot be considered statistically robust, as a single highly rated episode can substantially raise the overall average.

Second, the work of the Duffer Brothers provides the most informative case: they directed 23 episodes, with an average rating of 8.61. Given the substantially larger number of observations, this figure can be considered the most representative of the dataset. It suggests that the series’ core creative team maintained a consistently high level of audience reception across multiple seasons.

Interestingly, Shawn Levy, who directed nine episodes, suggested that his directorial approach was closely aligned with the series’ established style and creative vision.

One anomaly is particularly worth noting: the episode co-directed by the Duffer Brothers and Shawn Levy received a rating of 5.7 — the previously discussed Episode 7 from Season 5. Since this represents only a single episode, it cannot be considered representative of the directors’ overall work. However, it demonstrates that even within an otherwise consistent creative framework, individual episodes can perform poorly.

The Umbrella Academy

Interest in The Umbrella Academy remained at its lowest level from January 2016 through December 2018, even though plans to develop a series based on the comic books had been reported as early as July 2015, according to USA Today. The series itself premiered on February 15, 2019. This prolonged period of limited public attention reflects a common industry pattern: considerable time often passes between the initial announcement and the release of a finished series, while pre-release marketing activity may remain relatively limited.

Interest surged dramatically in August 2020, and several factors may help explain this spike. First, the second season was released on July 31, 2020, and went on to become particularly popular among fans. As Business Insider reported, the series even surpassed The Witcher in popularity during this period, indicating a strong audience response. Second, the broader context of 2020 cannot be overlooked: the COVID-19 pandemic and the resulting lockdowns substantially increased demand for streaming services. As Vulture noted, streaming platforms experienced exceptionally high levels of consumption during the summer of 2020, creating favorable conditions for increased viewership and online discussion. The August 2020 peak can therefore be understood as the result of at least two overlapping factors: the strong reception of the second season and the external circumstances that accelerated streaming consumption.

The data reveal a pronounced asymmetry in the distribution of audience interest: the mean search interest index is approximately 7.8, while the median is only 3. This suggests the series spent most of its lifespan attracting relatively limited attention, punctuated by rare but intense spikes in interest. Such a pattern is typical of series whose popularity is concentrated around particular events. In this case, one season clearly broke through, while subsequent seasons failed to generate comparable levels of audience interest. During quieter periods, the series retained a degree of audience loyalty but generated relatively little active engagement. The Umbrella Academy effectively surged during the pandemic but failed to sustain that momentum beyond it, as suggested by the progressively weaker peaks surrounding the releases of Seasons 3 and 4.

The episode with the highest number of votes was the pilot episode. In 2019, The Umbrella Academy was a new Netflix release that attracted significant attention through a variety of creative promotional campaigns and outdoor advertising initiatives surrounding its launch. For example, Netflix installed a giant umbrella featuring the series’ logo on Merewether Beach in Newcastle, Australia. According to Pedestrian, the structure measured more than 12 meters in width, 6 meters in height, and had a 14-meter-long handle.

Furthermore, these results provide additional support for the research hypothesis. Season 2 had the shortest average runtime at 47 minutes and received the highest average audience rating. In contrast, the longest season — and arguably the most resource-intensive in terms of production — Season 4, with an average runtime of 56 minutes, received the lowest rating. The data don’t indicate that viewers inherently dislike longer episodes; rather, the additional runtime appears not to have been filled with content capable of enhancing the overall quality of the series.

Here the Pearson correlation coefficient between episode runtime and average rating is −0.567. It indicates a moderate negative linear relationship: in most cases, as episode runtime increases, ratings tend to decrease rather than increase.

Conclusions

The findings support the research hypothesis

Increasing episode runtimes and expanding season scale do not necessarily increase audience engagement and may sometimes have the opposite effect.

Scale without quality leads to disappointment

In Stranger Things, the correlation between episode runtime and average rating is effectively zero, while The Umbrella Academy shows a moderate negative relationship. This suggests that longer episodes can reduce engagement when they result in slower pacing or an overstretched narrative. External factors also influence audience engagement. Both series experienced major peaks during the pandemic, when lockdowns increased demand for streaming content.

Marketing strategies can significantly increase audience attention

Promotional campaigns and social media activity can generate additional interest and viewership.

Final season is an event

Final seasons and episodes can attract high levels of audience participation because of their event status and nostalgia, rather than solely because of their quality or directorial choices.

Key Takeaway

One of the essential findings is the potential role of creative continuity in sustaining audience interest. Stranger Things, supported by the consistent creative leadership of the Duffers, maintained strong audience engagement over a decade. In contrast, The Umbrella Academy had a more fragmented directorial structure and experienced a decline in ratings and audience activity.

Overall, the findings suggest that even with large budgets, extensive marketing, and technological advances, the strongest factor in sustaining audience interest remains unique creative ideas and a distinctive authorial vision.
